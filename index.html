🌈 How it all works

🚽 Toilet cubicleA flat-pack plywood cubicle with a urine-diverting seat. Wee and poo are kept apart.
→
🪣 50 L steel bucketSolids plus a scoop of sawdust after each use. Swap the bucket when it is about ⅔ full (~25–30 kg).
→
⏳ Holding bayBuckets are emptied into a covered, lined bay where the material rests for 6–8 weeks. Ammonia gases off and it starts to break down.
→
🪱 Worm bedsFed in thin layers (5–8 cm) every 2–4 weeks across the warm months. Tiger worms turn it into vermicast.
→
🌳 Cure & useHarvested cast cures for 6 more months. Use it on trees and ornamentals, never on raw salad crops.

Urine goes separately to a container. Dilute it 1:10 and spread it on pasture or shelter trees, or run it to a soakage trench, subject to council rules.

Why the holding bay matters

Fresh humanure tipped straight onto worms in a thick layer heats itself up and releases ammonia. That kills the worms. Your input is one big month, followed by eleven quiet months. The holding bay soaks up the surge so the worms can be fed at a pace they can handle. The rest of the year they live on manure, cardboard and food scraps.

Why divert urine

Most of the smell, weight and ammonia comes from urine. Urine also contains salts that worms can't tolerate. Diverting it makes each bucket about 3× slower to fill, much lighter, nearly odourless, and safe to feed to worms.

Hunterville climate in short

Warm summers (maxima low–mid 20s °C, occasional 28–30 °C days). Cold, frosty winters (minima around 0–3 °C, frequent frosts June–August). Roughly 900–1100 mm of rain a year. Worms work best at 15–25 °C, slow right down below 10 °C, and die above about 30 °C or when frozen. Beds need shade, insulated lids and rain protection. (Approximate figures. Check NIWA CliFlo for your exact site.)

🚽 Plywood festival cubicle

Pick a character

Every cubicle is the same cheap flat-pack box. The character is just one extra ply sheet (a false front) cut to shape and screwed onto the front wall. You can mix a whole village and swap the fronts each year.

Design principles (cheap + good-looking)

  • 1.2 × 1.2 m footprint, sized to whole sheets. Each wall is one 2400 × 1200 sheet, so there are almost no cuts or offcuts.
  • Flat-pack panels. Each wall is a sheet screwed to a 90 × 45 perimeter frame. Panels bolt together at the corners with M10 coach bolts. It packs flat into a ute and stores dry for the other 11 months. Two people can put one up in about 20 minutes.
  • Skillion roof of clear or opal polycarbonate (e.g. Suntuf). It gives daylight inside, is cheap and light, and the fall to the back keeps rain off the door.
  • Raised floor on skids (two 100 × 75 H4 skids). No digging, it sits level on paddocks, and it can be dragged or forklifted.
  • Bucket access from the back. A hatch in the back wall lets crew swap buckets without going inside. It's quicker, more hygienic and keeps the queue moving.
  • Character fronts: the fun lives on one extra 12 mm sheet cut into a mushroom cap, rocket nose, castle battlements or chalet gable, screwed onto the front wall. It costs about $100–130 extra per cubicle, and the box underneath never changes.
  • Glow at night: back the cut-outs (spots, portholes, hearts) with offcuts of opal polycarbonate. They look white by day, and with a solar light inside they glow like lanterns after dark. Add a string of solar fairy lights along the roofline.
  • Bright paint, oiled inside: a bold colour and pattern outside (exterior acrylic sample pots are cheap for the dots and stripes), and natural oiled ply inside so it's light and wipeable.

Side walls are raked (2400 at the front → 2100 at the back). Each raked offcut becomes a roof gusset. The floor sheet's spare half makes the pedestal box.

Build steps

  1. Base: two 1500 mm H4 100 × 75 skids, with four 90 × 45 H3.2 joists across at 400 centres. Screw the 17 mm ply floor on top and seal its edges with paint.
  2. Wall frames: each wall is a 90 × 45 perimeter frame with one mid rail. Glue and screw the 12 mm ply on every 150 mm. Paint all edges before assembly, because end grain is where ply fails.
  3. Side walls: rake the top from 2400 (front) to 2100 (back). The cut line is a straight chalk line.
  4. Cut-out band: mark the motif 150–350 mm below the top on the front and side walls. Drill a 10 mm start hole and jigsaw it out. Staple fly mesh behind every opening (essential, because flies are the main way disease spreads).
  5. Front wall: rip the sheet into a 700 mm door and a 500 mm fixed panel. Frame the door, hang it on 3 tee hinges, and fit a self-closing spring hinge or bungee, a pull handle and an inside barrel bolt. An outside "vacant/engaged" slider is a nice touch.
  6. Back wall: cut a 550 × 600 hatch at the bottom, behind the pedestal, and hang it on hinges with a padlock hasp. Crew-only.
  7. Assemble: stand the walls, clamp, drill and bolt the corners with 4 × M10 × 120 coach bolts per corner. Bolt the walls to the floor.
  8. Roof: fit two 90 × 45 purlins on the raked tops and fix the polycarbonate with the proper roofing screws and washers. Overhang 150 mm at the back and sides, and 300 mm at the front over the door.
  9. Pedestal: build the 17 mm ply box (see section), fit the urine diverter and seat, and run the 25 mm urine hose out the back wall to the container. Fit a 100 mm PVC vent from the top of the box up past the roof by 300 mm, with fly mesh + cowl.
  10. Fit-out: sawdust bin + scoop, a toilet roll holder, a hook, a mirror, a small sign with the 3 rules, and a solar light. Put a hand-wash station outside (see below).
  11. Finish: exterior acrylic outside (2 coats), and decking oil or clear water-based poly inside so walls can be wiped down.
  12. Character front: draw a 100 mm grid on the extra sheet and copy the shape from the drawing above. Jigsaw it out, sand the edges, seal and paint them. Screw it to the front wall frame every 300 mm, with a 90 × 45 brace behind anything that sticks up more than 600 mm (rocket nose, flag). Rocket fins screw to the side-wall corners. Tall fronts catch wind, so stake every cubicle.
  13. Patterns: mask the stripes with tape. Do polka dots with a sponge roller cut into a circle, or a tin lid as a stencil. Do confetti freehand with sample pots (a great volunteer job).

Toilet rules sign (inside)

  1. Sit down, everyone (even for a wee). It keeps the wee in the front bowl.
  2. After a poo: 1 scoop of sawdust to cover it completely.
  3. Paper is fine. Nothing else: no wipes, pads, tampons, nappies, rubbish, cleaning sprays or water.
  4. Lid down, door shut, wash your hands.

Cheap fun extras

  • An engaged/vacant spinner: a ply disc on a bolt with 😊 / 😬 faces.
  • Label the sawdust bin "Magic Dust" or "Poo Glitter". People use it more.
  • A mirror with "you look great today" painted above it.

  • A "Poo-etry" corner: a laminated sheet of jokes or short poems, changed daily.
  • A sign about the worm farm: "Your donation feeds 10,000 worms 🪱". People love knowing where it goes.
  • Name every cubicle (🎲 above) so people can say "meet at the rocket".

Hand-wash station

A 20 L water container with a tap (or a foot-pump), a timber stand, a soap pump and a 20 L grey-water bucket underneath, emptied onto a gravel soak pit away from the beds. Allow about 1 station per 2 cubicles. Refill it every day and check the soap twice a day. Hand washing is the single most important health control on site.

🪱 Worm farm

Design

  • Lined timber bays, raised on concrete blocks, about 600 mm of bedding depth. Bay size and count come from the Planner.
  • Timber: use untreated macrocarpa (or other untreated timber) wherever wood touches bedding. CCA/ACQ-treated pine leaches copper and arsenic that harm worms and end up in the compost. Treated H4 timber is fine for the legs and the outer frame outside the liner.
  • Liner: 250 µm black polythene or pond liner, stapled to the inside walls. The floor falls 1:50 to a 20 mm tank outlet + tap at one corner. Leachate is collected, not let into the soil. That matters with human waste.
  • Drainage layer: 50 mm of coarse wood chip, with shade cloth on top so bedding doesn't block it.
  • Lid: 12 mm ply with 50 mm polystyrene glued underneath. It keeps out rain and frost in winter and heat in summer. Hinge it at the back, and fit a prop stick and a fly-meshed vent slot.
  • Rodent proofing: fit 6 mm galvanised mesh over vents, and keep the lid tight-fitting.
  • Site: full or afternoon shade (under deciduous trees is ideal: shade in summer, sun in winter). Keep it out of hollows that flood and sheltered from the prevailing westerly. Allow at least 20 m from streams/drains and 50 m from bores (confirm with Horizons).

Worms

  • Tiger worms (Eisenia fetida) and red worms (Lumbricus rubellus / Dendrobaena), sold by NZ worm farms by the kg. Garden earthworms won't live in compost.
  • Start the beds at least 3 months before the first festival on aged horse/cow manure + shredded cardboard. The population roughly doubles every 2–3 months in good conditions.
  • Stock at about 1 kg/m² minimum (2 kg/m² is better). The Planner shows how much to buy.
  • Ideal conditions: 15–25 °C, as moist as a wrung-out sponge, pH 6.5–7.5, dark, and plenty of air.
  • Year-round food: aged horse or cow manure (never from animals drenched in the last 4–6 weeks, because ivermectin-type dewormers in dung kill worms), cardboard, leaves, coffee grounds, and festival food scraps (no meat, dairy, citrus, onion or oil).

How to feed the toilet material

  1. Only feed material that has rested 6–8 weeks in the holding bay and no longer smells strongly of ammonia. Sniff test: earthy or mild is OK, while sharp, eye-watering or rotten is not ready.
  2. Spread it in a 5–8 cm layer over half the bed only. The worms then have a safe half to retreat to if it heats up.
  3. Mix in a little shredded cardboard if it's wet, and cover with damp cardboard or straw.
  4. Check after 3–5 days. If worms have moved into it, good. If the layer is hot (above 30 °C on a probe thermometer), don't add more.
  5. Only add the next layer once the last one is mostly eaten (2–4 weeks in summer, 6–8+ in winter). Use the monthly plan as a guide.

Cut list (per bay)

Includes a single holding bay of the same construction (1.2 × 1.2 × 1.0 m) in the Planner's count. It's built like a worm bay, just deeper and with no worms.

Harvesting

Twice a year, best in late spring and late summer. Stop feeding one end of a bay for 6 weeks and feed only the other end, so worms migrate across. Then shovel out the finished end. Alternatively, tip the cast into heaps in bright light; worms dive down, and you scrape off the top. Cure the harvested cast in a covered heap or bags for 6 more months (12 months total from toilet to use is the safety target). Sieve it through 6–10 mm mesh and return any worms to the bed.

The barrel is a catchment, not a treatment plant. It keeps solids, fat and food out of the paddock, and the soil does the rest. Never run toilet waste or urine through it.

🧮 Size it

Rough rules of thumb: about 100 L/day per barrel before the chip layer blinds up too fast, and trench soakage of 5–25 L per m² of trench floor per day depending on soil. Pipe fall needs to be at least 1:40 (25 mm per metre). Watch how it behaves on day one and add a second barrel if water sits on top for more than an hour.

Inside the barrel (top → bottom)

  1. Lid: the drum's clamp lid, or ply with fly mesh. It keeps rain, kids, dogs and flies out. The inlet pipe goes through it.
  2. Air gap ~150 mm: room for a dump of water to pond for a moment.
  3. Splash plate: a paver or tile under the inlet so the stream doesn't dig a hole in the chip.
  4. Untreated wood chip or bark, 100 mm: the "sacrificial" layer. It catches the fat and food. Rake it daily and swap it when it goes slimy.
  5. Shade cloth or geotextile disc: lets you lift the chip off without taking the sand with it.
  6. Coarse washed sand, 400 mm: concrete or "plasterer's coarse" sand. Not fine builder's sand, and not beach sand (salt).
  7. Pea gravel 7–10 mm, 100 mm: stops sand washing into the gravel below.
  8. Drainage gravel 20 mm, 100 mm: around the outlet fitting.
  9. Outlet: a 50 mm tank outlet (bulkhead) fitting, 50 mm above the drum floor, into the slotted coil.

Build steps

  1. Pick the spot downhill of the kitchen, at least 20 m from any stream, drain or water trough, and away from where people camp or walk.
  2. Drill the barrel side with a hole saw 50 mm up from the floor, fit the tank outlet with its seals, and silicone it. Fill it with a bucket of water to check it doesn't leak.
  3. Dig the hole about 150 mm wider than the barrel and to the burial depth. Dig the trench from the outlet across the paddock along the contour, 450 mm wide, with an almost level floor (a slight fall away is fine). This spreads the water along its full length.
  4. Stand the barrel on a bed of gravel and level it. Push the slotted, socked coil onto the outlet, lay it in the trench on 100 mm of gravel, cover it with 200 mm more gravel and shade cloth, then put the soil and turf back. Cap the far end.
  5. Fill the barrel with the layers above, then backfill around the outside.
  6. At the sink, fit the basket strainer waste and the 40 mm trap. Use the adaptor to go onto the unslotted coil. Run it to the barrel with a steady fall and no dips, through a hole in the lid, ending 50 mm above the splash plate. Peg the coil every metre so it can't sag.
  7. Test: pour 3 buckets of water down the sink. It should drain freely, nothing should pond above the chip after a few minutes, and the trench end should stay dry on top.

Keep it working

  • Fat is the enemy. Wipe greasy pans and plates with paper towel or bread into the bin before washing, and never pour hot fat or oil down the sink. Collect it in a tin and take it to waste.
  • Empty the sink strainer into the food scraps bin after every wash-up. Vegetable scraps can go to the worm farm (no meat, dairy, citrus, onion or oil).
  • Use biodegradable, phosphate-free dish liquid. No bleach, caustic drain cleaners or disinfectant down the sink.
  • Every morning: lift the lid, rake the chip, and check for ponding. If water sits on top for more than an hour, swap the chip layer.
  • Have a bag of spare chip on site, at about 30 L per barrel for every 3–4 days.

After the festival

  • Let the barrel drain for a day, then lift off the chip layer and the top 50 mm of sand and add them to the toilet holding bay or a hot compost heap. Never put them in the worm beds directly, because of the fat.
  • Top up with fresh sand, put the lid on and leave the barrel in the ground for next year. Mark the trench line with a waratah so nobody drives over it.
  • Replace all the sand every 2–3 years, or sooner if it drains slowly.

Rules

Kitchen wash water counts as greywater. Under Horizons' One Plan, small greywater discharges to land are usually allowed when they soak in below the surface, don't pond or run off, and are kept well back from water. Confirm the setback with Horizons and include the sand trap in the event plan. Never let it run onto the surface or into a drain or stream.

🧤 Safety, health & rules

Pathogens: why handling and timing matter

Human faeces can carry E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter, norovirus, Hepatitis A, Giardia, Cryptosporidium and worm eggs (e.g. Ascaris, the hardiest). Vermicomposting reduces them a lot but doesn't sterilise. Time is the main safety factor: aim for 12 months or more from the toilet to using the compost. Use it only around trees, shrubs, ornamentals and pasture margins, not on vegetables eaten raw, and not within a month of grazing.

Crew handling rules

  • Wear nitrile or heavy rubber gloves, long sleeves, and closed shoes or gumboots. Wear eye protection when tipping buckets.
  • Two people per full bucket, or use a sack trolley. A full 50 L bucket can weigh 40+ kg, which is why the planner fills to ~65%.
  • Use a dedicated "dirty" wheelbarrow, shovel and fork, painted a different colour, and never use them for food areas.
  • Wash hands with soap after every handling session and before eating or smoking.
  • Keep vaccinations current: tetanus, and consider Hepatitis A for regular handlers.
  • Keep a spill kit: sawdust, a shovel, bags, gloves and diluted bleach for hard surfaces only (never into the buckets or beds).
  • Anyone with diarrhoea or vomiting should stay off handling duty. Report gastro outbreaks on site to the festival medic and the public health unit.

Steel buckets

  • Galvanised steel corrodes from urine salts and acidic compost. The zinc ends up in the compost and the buckets rust through within a few seasons. Prefer stainless (food-grade 304), or epoxy-paint the inside of galvanised buckets.
  • Put a 3–5 cm layer of sawdust in the bottom of every empty bucket. It stops sticking and soaks up liquid.
  • Use tight lids for transport. Never stack full buckets more than 2 high.
  • Wash after emptying: hose them out into the holding bay, scrub, and leave them upside down in the sun to dry.
  • Number every bucket and log its fill date, so the holding bay can be loaded in order.

Council & regional rules (check before building)

  • Rangitīkei District Council: composting toilets are normally covered by the NZ Building Code (clauses G1 Personal hygiene and G13 Foul water) and the AS/NZS 1546.2 standard for waterless composting toilets. Temporary event toilets may be handled under the event permit instead. Ask the council's building and environmental health teams early.
  • Horizons Regional Council (One Plan): discharging human waste, urine or leachate to land has setback and volume conditions. The lined bays with collected leachate are designed to make compliance easy, but confirm before the first event.
  • Event health requirements usually set the number of toilets per attendee and the hand-wash requirements. Your festival's event management plan should reference this system.
  • Keep a simple log book (bucket counts, holding-bay dates, feeding dates, harvest dates, where compost was used). Council officers like it, and it makes troubleshooting easy.

This page is planning guidance, not legal advice. Rules and setback distances differ by site, so confirm them with both councils.
